LevoZ Labs · Product summary

Closet

Show it a few pieces you already think look good. It works out what they have in common, goes and finds real things to buy that extend it, throws out everything that doesn’t fit, and hangs what’s left in a wardrobe you can come back to.

The problem it actually solves

Most people know what they like when they see it and cannot describe it. Ask someone their style and you get “casual, I guess”; show them two jackets and they will pick the right one instantly. Every shopping tool built so far asks for the description.

Closet asks for the pictures. Taste goes in as evidence rather than vocabulary, and what comes back is not inspiration or a mood board — it is specific listings, in stock, in your size, at a price you set, with a link.

How a run works

  1. Upload

    One to six photos, downscaled in the browser and sent straight to the model. Nothing is uploaded to a storage service; the photos are never hosted anywhere.

  2. Read the style

    A vision pass reads aesthetic, palette, silhouette, fabrics and formality across the set, works out what is missing rather than what is already there, and writes eight to ten specific shopping queries. “Brown suede chelsea boot”, not “men’s shoes”.

  3. Search

    Every query runs against eBay and Google Shopping at once, scoped to menswear categories resolved live rather than hardcoded. Results are deduped, interleaved so no single query or source can crowd out the others, and anything whose title states a size that cannot fit is dropped before the model sees it.

  4. Choose

    Three passes run in parallel over slices of the pool, each looking at the actual photographs. A listing captioned “waxed cotton jacket” showing a woman’s cropped jacket is dropped on sight. Each surviving piece gets one sentence on why it suits you and is tagged by category, maker, material and colour.

Why it is different from a recommendation feed

  • The photograph is the evidence, the title is a claim

    Every candidate is judged on its actual product image. Sellers mislabel constantly, and on secondhand marketplaces the picture is the only honest part of a listing.

  • A wide net, a tight result

    It gathers around 120 candidates and shows at most nine. Being selective requires having something to select between, and the value is in what gets thrown away.

  • It has to fit

    Sizes are asked for once and remembered. A beautiful jacket two sizes wrong is worth nothing, and no amount of watching someone browse ever reveals their inseam.

  • Secondhand is a first-class source

    Most of what is genuinely good at a given price has already been owned once. Searching eBay on relevance rather than price is what surfaces it.

  • The result is an object, not a page

    Pieces hang in a wardrobe that builds itself as the run completes, each in a garment bag that clears when you look at it. The closet is saved under a short code, so it is somewhere you return to rather than a list you scroll once.

What it learns

Every closet is scored against you without asking you to fill anything in. Each piece counts as an impression; opening it, clicking through to the listing, and answering Right for you? are progressively stronger signals. Those accumulate against the piece’s material, colour, category and maker — and maker within a category, because someone excellent at waxed jackets has told you nothing about their boots.

The care is in not over-reading it. One click on three impressions is not a 33% preference, it is noise, so an attribute has to be seen five times before it counts at all, and what it reports is a confidence interval measured against your own average rather than a raw rate. What survives that goes into both the searching and the choosing on every later run, so the wardrobe moves toward you instead of repeating itself.

All of it is per browser, under an anonymous identifier. There are no accounts, no sign-up, and no email required to use the product at all.

The app runs without Upstash or SerpAPI — those are enhancements, not requirements. Google Shopping is wired and behind a key; its response mapping has not yet been confirmed against live results end to end.

Economics

Roughly $0.20–0.30 in model cost per full run, almost all of it in the two vision passes. That figure predates splitting the judging into parallel batches and has not been re-measured since.

There is no revenue path connected yet. The obvious one is affiliate: eBay pays 4% on fashion, and because the product already produces a small list of specific listings that someone asked for, the click-through it generates is unusually qualified. Wiring it is a header and one field on the search call — the work is the programme sign-up, not the code.

What is worth building next

Prove the second source. Retail listings alongside secondhand roughly doubles the pool and is the difference between a marketplace tool and a shopping product. It is wired; it needs one live run against real results.

Connect the revenue. Affiliate links are the smallest piece of work here with the largest change in what the product is.

Carry taste across devices. Everything learned is tied to one browser. The closet code already travels; the taste behind it does not.
